Near-death experiences in Iran

Near-death experiences (NDEs) are usually described in the research literature from Western samples: a sense of leaving the body, a tunnel or light, meeting dead relatives, a life review, a feeling of peace or, less often, distress. Iran offers a different setting for the same kind of report. There, accounts are told and interpreted inside an existing Islamic picture of death.

The frame: barzakh

Islamic tradition says that after death a person enters the barzakh, an interval before the resurrection in which the soul is already rewarded or punished. Shiʿi thought adds a rich account of the Imams' presence at the hour of death and of the angel of death. In the philosophical school of Mullā Ṣadrā (d. 1640), the barzakh is the "imaginal world" (ʿālam-i mithāl), where a person meets the forms of his own deeds. This gives Iranian narrators and listeners ready terms for what they report, and it shapes what is told: Imams or Fāṭima as helpers, the angel of death, valleys of reckoning, hell shown to the living, the dead who send the experiencer back.

Where the accounts circulate

  • Television. The state broadcaster's programme Zendegi pas az zendegi ("Life after life", IRIB Channel 4, presented by Abbas Mowzoon) has run for several seasons and has been studied by Iranian and international researchers (for example in Omega 2024 and in papers from 2023 and 2025) as a large pool of NDE accounts in Persian.
  • Books. Popular collections such as Ān sūy-e marg, whose three accounts are the subject of this site, have had wide readership.
  • Preaching. Clerics such as Aminikhah teach with these accounts, not so much to prove survival after death as to turn the listener to a moral lesson: repent, repay people, keep sincerity, hold to certainty.

What stands out

Features known from the global literature appear: separation from the body, a tunnel, a being of light, life review, return. Other features are strongly shaped by the setting: accountability for wrongs to other people (the "valley of the rights of others" in the third story here), punishment scenes shown to the experiencer, named religious figures and a strong theme of being sent back to repair one's life. Distressing experiences are reported more openly than in many Western collections, and they are used for preaching.

A note of caution

These are testimonies, told and retold through media and sermons; the accounts in Ān sūy-e marg are given by anonymous narrators and edited by the authors. The lectures presented here take them as true and explain them theologically. A reader can use them as a primary source for how an Islamic community understands and teaches NDEs, without taking a position on the experiences themselves. The summaries on this site separate what the narrators report from what Aminikhah claims.
