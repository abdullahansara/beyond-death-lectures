How a Ṣadrian seminary thinker reads NDEs

The research literature on Iranian near-death experiences (reviewed here) mostly reports what experiencers say. These lectures are valuable for a different reason: they show how a seminary-trained, Ṣadrian-leaning cleric with institutional ties to the Leader's office interprets such accounts for a lay and student audience. He is described as the Leader's representative at the Faculty of Engineering of Ferdowsi University of Mashhad, and the book he lectures on was published by Nashr-i Maʿārif, an institution of the same office. The lectures give a sustained, unguarded and often humorous statement of a position that is otherwise seen only in short academic or media pieces. What follows draws together the main points of the series, with links to the sessions where each is made.

1. Status of the accounts: testimony that confirms, not proof

  • He treats the three narratives as credible testimony whose truth he cannot demonstrate, and says so (17, 18). Their value is checked by comparison with the Qurʾān and the Imams' sayings, not by empirical test (6).
  • The purpose is moral and epistemic: to produce yaqīn, certainty, which he calls the rarest provision and the thing to ask for on the Night of Power (39). The question "is it true?" gives way to "what does it do to the listener?"
  • Dreams and visions are "discoverers" (kāshif), not proof (ḥujja); a dream that contradicts reason or revelation is to be rejected (41). Even the saints are guarded against being deceived by good-looking suggestions, and he is wary of movements built on dreams.
  • He distrusts the Western label "metaphysics", which he says treats the unseen as something to be ignored, and prefers "the world of the unseen" (40).

2. Ontology: a Ṣadrian account of what the experiencer meets

  • Imaginal body and imaginal world. The experiencer leaves a material body and acts in a subtle, "imaginal" body with form but no matter (6, 7, 9). He distinguishes the attached image (one's own imagination) from the detached image (the outer imaginal world of angels and spirits) and says they are alike in kind (22).
  • Substantial motion and the absence of change. Matter is the world of potentiality and change; above it there is displacement but no decay (10, 25). This explains pleasure without defect, fruits that are in two places, and water that does not wet (28, 30).
  • Time. There is no time as we know it in the barzakh; a day here corresponds to a vast span there, and the doctor's 135,000 years are read this way (10, 17, 22).
  • The soul and the body. The soul is not in the body but the body in the soul; the brain is the radiation of the soul's perception and the intellect continues without it (28).
  • Primacy of existence and unity of being. Things are manifestations of the divine names; "God is everything" is acceptable, "everything is God" is not (25, 37).
  • Deed and doer are one. The decisive idea: what a person sees in the barzakh is the form of his own deeds, thoughts and traits. The valley of the rights of others, the animals shown to the damned, the paradise gardens and the house of light are all read as manifestations of the self (17, 18, 22, 27). Punishment is therefore not an external act of vengeance but the unremovable form of what one has become.

3. Differences between experiences are differences of rank

Why does the first narrator meet ʿAlī in light, the second see devils and hungry spirits, and the third spend 135,000 years among creditors? He answers with hierarchies: levels of intellect and sincerity (28, 33), Ṭabāṭabāʾī's three hells and paradises of deeds, traits and essence (17, 21), and higher standards for scholars, so that what earns credit for an ordinary person can be "rejected" for the great (38). The same scheme explains why martyrs and the sincere (mukhlaṣūn) differ from the ordinary dead (40, 41). This is a theory of why NDE content varies without appeal to culture as such.

4. What matters morally: rights of others, unity, sincerity, and no attachment

  • Rights of others (ḥaqq al-nās) are placed at the centre of the third story, with a practical casuistry of repayment (19, 20, 40).
  • Tawḥīd and shirk are treated as matters of degree that include loving wealth as much as God, ostentation in worship and treating means as independent causes (37).
  • Paradise is not the goal. He repeatedly mocks a picture of paradise as pools and roast chicken, and says the aim is nearness to God and the manifestation of all the divine names (4, 25).
  • Attachment to rank, wealth and the body is the substance of barzakhi suffering (8, 14).

5. What he rejects

  • Reincarnation. He argues from the Ṣadrian principle that what has become actual never returns to potentiality (11, 41).
  • The universalist reading that all religions' afterlives are the same is only partly accepted; he cites the doctor's remark that the scriptures converge (31) but anchors all of it in Shiʿi sources.
  • Dwelling on the jinn. He tells listeners not to read the book's last forty pages and warns of the effect of such material on the imagination (13, 40).

6. How NDE material is joined to politics and eschatology

In the closing sessions the afterlife account is linked to the Imam of the Age's appearance, to sorcery and to current events (36, 40, 41). He reads recent political events as signs, praises the Leader's judgement, and recommends a particular lineage of teachers. These passages are data about the setting in which NDE accounts are used in an institutionally connected Shiʿi preaching: the experience serves as moral formation and as part of a wider vision of history. These are his own opinions.

7. Method and style as evidence

He addresses engineers and scientists in an informal register, uses mathematical and everyday analogies (the pen and the hand, the sun and mirrors, packets of milk), and quotes ʿAllāma Ṭabāṭabāʾī's al-Mīzān and Mullā Ṣadrā's Asfār through Ayatollah Jawādī Āmulī's teaching. This makes the series a good specimen of how Ṣadrian philosophy is turned into popular pastoral teaching in post-revolutionary Iran, and of how it is used to give NDEs a theological home.
